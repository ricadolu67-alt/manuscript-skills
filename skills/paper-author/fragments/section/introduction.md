# Introduction

Do not default to "background, literature review, gap, contribution". In the shortest distance possible, the reader should learn what the question is, what the answer is, why it is true, and why it is new.

## Openings: pick by the material, compare two

- **Open problem:** a question that the predecessor or the classical theory explicitly left open. Common in theory papers.
- **Concrete surprise:** a counter-intuitive fact in the simplest setting, followed by the question of why it happens. A good choice when the headline is a general characterization.
- **Counterexample:** a natural conjecture or a successful old model that fails in one regime, which points to the missing ingredient.
- **A computation or model that fails:** a plausible result that violates a key structure. Common in numerical-method papers.
- **The null result the effect breaks:** a symmetry, cancellation or classical zero that makes the naive answer "nothing happens". Common in mechanism papers.
- **An observation:** a phenomenon that existing mechanisms cannot explain. Common in experimental papers.
- **A decision under pressure:** what current practice costs in a specific operating condition. Common in engineering papers.

## What the introduction must deliver

1. The question is concrete by the end of the first or second paragraph.
2. **The answer comes early.**
   - Theory papers usually add a main-results passage or list in which each `DECISIVE` result carries its formula and points to its theorem.
   - A short notation paragraph before that passage defines every symbol its formulas use.
3. **One mechanism paragraph** explains why the answer holds, before any proof.
4. **Prior work is set out as scientific relations:** where existing results work, where they stop, and what this paper changes. Use a comparison table when there are several settings. Never describe a precedent as ruled out unless the ledger says it was checked.
5. Contributions are framed as changes in understanding, not as completed tasks.

## Check

After the first two pages, can a reader restate the main result and say why it is new in one sentence? If not, move the answer forward.
