# Editing guide: mature academic English

The aim is accurate, natural prose with a reading rhythm. It is not a claim about native-speaker identity, and it does not promise that every editor would agree.

## Whole sections before sentences

When a section needs restructuring, first note for yourself, in a few words per paragraph, its scientific object and its inferential job. Then move and merge paragraphs accordingly, rewrite the joins, and only then polish sentences.

Finish one topic before turning to the next: its definition, mechanism, criterion and evidence. Return to an earlier topic only when new evidence forces it, and say why. After any move, reread the sentences on both sides of the cut and fix openings, endings and references. "The three loads above" no longer works once a methods paragraph sits in between.

## Information and syntax

- Known material usually opens a sentence and new material earns the end. Change that order deliberately when you want a result or counterexample to stand out.
- The subject should be what changes, compares or acts. A concrete model, quantity or operation is clearer than a chain of abstract nouns. Accurate passives in proofs and methods are fine.
- Long sentences carry tightly linked conditions or reasoning; short ones close, turn or reveal a consequence. Adjust for cognitive load, with no fixed rhythm and no length thresholds.
- A connective should name a real relation: cause, contrast, consequence, scale. When the reasoning already shows the relation, no connective is needed. Do not use "Furthermore" or "Importantly" to cover a missing link.
- Keep distinctions exact: estimate versus bound, association versus mechanism, sufficient versus necessary, simulation versus measurement. Keep terms stable; do not vary the name of one quantity for style.

## Paragraphs and emphasis

- Put the result a peer should remember where the reader can understand it.
- Make the comparison, the direction of the effect and the consequence clear before choosing the numbers that support them.
- A control parameter, an error that could change the conclusion, or a counterexample stays next to the claim it affects.
- Delete sentences that only restate the previous one. Keep explanations that make a new object, equation or piece of evidence intelligible.
- Use verbs as strong as the evidence: direct results get direct verbs, and inferred mechanisms keep their uncertainty. Do not turn every "show" into "may suggest", and do not strengthen a verb to compensate for weak evidence.

## Where qualifiers go

| Qualifier affects | Place it |
|---|---|
| Whether a specific claim is true | In the claim's sentence or theorem; later references point back to it |
| The whole paper's model, observable or accounting | Once, where it is defined |
| A specific number | Next to that number or in its caption |
| Interpretation, when rival explanations remain | Where the interpretation is made, with the most discriminating evidence |
| Extensions beyond scope | The Discussion, only if they have scientific consequences |
| Internal workflow and tooling | Never in the text |

The same condition may be merged into one place. Different conditions must not be deleted to look concise.

## Final comparison

Compare the edited text with the input once. Check that facts, conditions, negative results, signs, numbers, definitions and citations were not prettified into something else, and that each paragraph's job is clear. Report only real changes and genuine input gaps.
